# Findings: payment gridlock and liquidity hoarding

_Synthetic scenario. Results are conditional on model assumptions and are not forecasts of real incidents. Generated by `experiments/gridlock_and_hoarding.py` (engine 0.4.0); every number below comes from the CSV files next to this file._

Metric: overdue value × hours (the sum over hours of the value of payments that are due but unpaid). Lower is less disruption; it is not a loss estimate.

## 1. Gridlock: when does multilateral offsetting help?

- **Circular trade (cluster), 12-hour outage, balances as configured:** offsetting removes 38% of the disruption with within-step recycling and 41% with next-step recycling. In both it settles the suppliers' ring of 7 payments in one batch, and EUR 72,000 of the gross value needs no cash at all.
- **It depends on cash in a non-monotone way** (12-hour outage; reduction by balance factor):

| Balance factor | 0.5× | 0.75× | 0.9× | 1× | 1.25× | 1.5× | 2× | 3× |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| within step | 0% | 0% | 0% | 38% | 19% | 24% | 6% | 0% |
| next step | 0% | 3% | 6% | 41% | 43% | 41% | 44% | 0% |

  With too little cash, the participants who end the ring as net payers cannot cover even their net positions: the greedy rule removes their payments, the ring unravels and nothing settles (0%). With ample cash, firms pay one another directly and there is no gridlock left to resolve. Offsetting helps only in a middle band.
- **One-directional supply chain:** across all 64 tested combinations of balance factor, recycling rule and outage duration, offsetting formed 0 batches. Its delays come from routes that are down and from cash that is genuinely missing, not from payments waiting for one another.
- **Across 300 random test structures:** offsetting formed at least one batch in 78; the disruption was lower in 77, unchanged in 222 and higher in 1. Settlement is path-dependent: settling a batch early can move cash away from a participant who needed it for a later payment. These shares describe the generated structures only.

## 2. Hoarding: does holding cash back for urgent payments help?

- **Under stress the rule made the network's disruption worse in 27 of 28 tested settings** (two scenarios × protected priority 1 or 2 × 7 look-ahead windows). Change against no rule: cluster +0% to +24%, supply chain +0% to +81%.
- **Urgent payments themselves** (overdue value × hours of priority-1 payments, stress): cluster lower in 12, unchanged in 2 and higher in 0 settings; supply chain lower in 7, unchanged in 2 and higher in 5.
- **A longer window is not simply more protection** (supply chain, priority ≤ 1): with a 24-hour look-ahead the urgent payments are worse off than with no rule (EUR 431,000·h against EUR 235,000·h), while 48 hours brings them to EUR 0·h, at +81% network disruption. Whether a firm's own reserve protects its payroll depends on what everyone else holds back.
- **A longer window is not simply more protection** (supply chain, priority ≤ 2): with a 1-hour look-ahead the urgent payments are worse off than with no rule (EUR 260,000·h against EUR 235,000·h), while 48 hours brings them to EUR 60,000·h, at +44% network disruption.
- **Even without an outage** the rule creates delays in 14 of 28 tested settings: firms hold cash back from payments that would otherwise have settled on time.
- **The rule can cancel other interventions** (cluster, 12-hour outage, the scenario's candidates in pairs): liquidity buffer + payroll-protection reserve ends 6% worse than no intervention, against 45% removed if they acted independently (12 payments held back although they could be funded); multilateral offsetting + payroll-protection reserve ends 12% worse than no intervention, against 30% removed if they acted independently (6 payments held back although they could be funded, 0 offsetting batches). A buffer's cash is kept back for the recipient's own tax and payroll instead of paying its suppliers, and offsetting finds no covered set once every firm must also keep its payroll cash.
- **Cluster: one firm alone against everyone** (stress, priority 1, 24-hour look-ahead): 4 of 8 firms with urgent payments reduce their own urgent delay by applying the rule alone; of those, 2 are no better off than with no rule once every firm applies it. Network disruption: EUR 2,081,500·h with no rule, EUR 2,331,900·h when everyone applies it.
- **Supply chain: one firm alone against everyone** (stress, priority 1, 24-hour look-ahead): 2 of 12 firms with urgent payments reduce their own urgent delay by applying the rule alone; of those, 1 is no better off than with no rule once every firm applies it. Network disruption: EUR 1,855,000·h with no rule, EUR 3,190,400·h when everyone applies it.
- **Across 300 random test structures** (priority 1, 12-hour look-ahead, every participant): total disruption lower in 29, unchanged in 4, higher in 267; priority-1 overdue value × hours lower in 265, unchanged in 21, higher in 14. In 237 structures the rule protected urgent payments at the cost of more total disruption.

## Limits

- Two hand-built scenarios and generated test structures: none is calibrated to real firms, payment systems or incidents, and the random structures have no economic meaning.
- Offsetting uses one greedy removal rule (the last payment of the participant with the largest uncovered position goes first). It does not search for the largest settleable set; an optimising algorithm could settle more.
- The reserve rule is one stylised treasury behaviour with perfect knowledge of the firm's own calendar. Firms do not learn, negotiate, borrow or cancel payments.
- Nothing here estimates probabilities, losses or returns; counts over random structures are not likelihoods.
